\section{Phase 5: Comparative Analysis, Ablations, and Failure Analysis}

Deliverable~2 established that the learned field predicts $h^\ast$ accurately in an
average squared-error sense and that clipping it to Manhattan distance keeps A$^\ast$
near-optimal. That result, however, does not answer the question the project set out to
test: does the learned heuristic \emph{reduce search effort} relative to Manhattan? This
phase answers that question with paired non-parametric tests, isolates which input
features carry the signal, and explains the observed failure mechanistically. The
headline finding is negative: the learned field does not improve search efficiency in
this configuration, and on the easiest mazes it is dramatically worse. The value of the
analysis is in showing \emph{why} a well-fitting regressor can still be a poor heuristic.

\subsection{Research Questions and Analysis Protocol}

We address four questions:
\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{RQ1 (effect).} Does the neural heuristic expand fewer nodes than
  Manhattan A$^\ast$ across the 282 test mazes?
  \item \textbf{RQ2 (components).} Which feature groups are responsible for the
  regression signal, and does removing them change search behaviour?
  \item \textbf{RQ3 (mechanism).} If search gets worse, is the cause local regression
  error, heuristic inconsistency, distance-dependent bias, or a mismatch between the
  training objective and the search objective?
  \item \textbf{RQ4 (reproducibility).} Do the shipped model artefacts reproduce the
  reported numbers, and can the PyTorch pipeline be used for the benchmark?
\end{enumerate}

All comparisons are paired by maze using the key \texttt{(file\_name, local\_index)}.
Nodes expanded is the primary metric. We report the mean paired difference, a percentile
bootstrap 95\% confidence interval (5{,}000 resamples), the two-sided Wilcoxon
signed-rank test, and win/tie/loss counts. The analysis is implemented in
\texttt{src/final\_analysis.py} and driven by \texttt{scripts/failure\_analysis.py};
every number and figure in this section is regenerated by those scripts from the
committed evaluation CSV.

\subsection{RQ2: Component Ablation}

To determine whether the failure is a capacity problem or a representation problem, we
retrained the same MLP architecture on seven feature subsets: all 29 features, each
group removed, and each group in isolation. Training used 100{,}000 cells, the test
metrics used 60{,}000 held-out cells, and search impact was measured on a common
\PfiveAblMazes{}-maze test subset. Table~\ref{tab:feature_ablation} reports the result.

Three conclusions follow.
\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{Goal offsets carry essentially all of the signal.} Removing the two
  goal-relative features collapses $R^2$ from \PfiveAblAllRtwo{} to \PfiveAblNoGoalRtwo{},
  while keeping only those two features retains \PfiveAblGoalOnlyRtwo{}. Coordinates and
  the wall window are almost redundant for regression fidelity.
  \item \textbf{Wall context helps regression slightly but hurts search.} Removing the
  $5\times5$ window costs only $0.01$ in $R^2$, yet the full model expands
  \PfiveAblAllNodes{} nodes on the ablation subset versus \PfiveAblGoalOnlyNodes{} for
  the goal-offset-only model. The wall window lets the network fit local obstacle
  patterns, but the resulting field is less smooth globally.
  \item \textbf{Even the best variant is worse than Manhattan.} The goal-offset-only
  model expands \PfiveAblGoalOnlyNodes{} nodes against
  \PfiveAblManhattanNodes{} for Manhattan on the same subset. Better regression fidelity
  does not translate into better search.
\end{enumerate}

\textbf{RQ2 answer:} the model is not under-parameterised; the useful information is
almost entirely the goal direction. The additional wall features improve average
regression but make the field less useful to A$^\ast$.

\subsection{RQ3: Why the Field Hurts Search}

\subsubsection{Heuristic Inconsistency}

\subsubsection{Distance-Dependent Bias}

The residuals are not zero-mean. Pooled over 48 mazes and 125{,}916 free cells, the raw
field has MAE \PfiveFieldRawMae{} cells and bias \PfiveFieldRawBias{} cells, with
\PfiveFieldRawOver{}\% of cells overestimated and only \PfiveFieldRawWithin{}\% within
one cell of the truth. The sign of the error depends on the distance to the goal
(Figure~\ref{fig:phase5_distance}): near the goal the raw field overestimates by
\PfiveDistanceNearBias{} cells, while in the farthest distance bin it underestimates by
\PfiveDistanceFarBias{} cells. Clipping eliminates overestimation but turns the field
into a systematically optimistic estimate, with bias \PfiveFieldClipBias{} cells and
\PfiveFieldClipUnder{}\% of cells underestimated; the farthest-bin bias reaches
\PfiveClipDistanceFar{} cells.

This is the central mechanism. Near the goal, a small overestimate can close off the
corridor that contains the optimal path, forcing A$^\ast$ to explore alternatives.
Far from the goal, a large underestimate gives A$^\ast$ almost no gradient, so it
expands a broad wavefront. Clipping fixes the first problem by construction but
amplifies the second: it replaces the learned field with $\min(\hat h, h_{\text{man}})$,
so on mazes where the true cost is far above Manhattan, the clipped field is identical
to Manhattan and cannot help.

\centering

\caption{Signed residual by true cost-to-goal. The raw field crosses from

\subsubsection{Dead Ends and Wall Adjacency}

Errors concentrate where the topology is locally ambiguous. Dead-end cells have MAE
\PfiveDeadMae{} cells versus \PfiveOtherMae{} for other free cells, and their bias is
more negative (\PfiveDeadBias{} versus \PfiveOtherBias{}), although they are
overestimated less often (\PfiveDeadOver{}\% versus \PfiveOtherOver{}). The MAE rises
monotonically with the number of wall neighbours, from \PfiveWallMaeLow{} cells with no
wall neighbour to \PfiveWallMaeHigh{} cells with three, while the bias deepens from
\PfiveWallBiasLow{} to \PfiveWallBiasHigh{} cells. The network is uncertain precisely
where obstacle geometry determines the remaining cost.

This pattern is consistent with the feature ablation: a $5\times5$ window contains the
local wall pattern but not the global corridor structure that determines whether a dead
end is a short detour or a long one. The model therefore produces locally plausible but
globally inconsistent estimates.

Table~\ref{tab:phase5_strata} and Figure~\ref{fig:phase5_groups} stratify the raw
neural/Manhattan expansion ratio by environment. The pattern is the opposite of the

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Density.} The ratio falls from \PfiveDensityTenRatio$\times$ at 10\%
  obstacle density to \PfiveDensityFortyRatio$\times$ at 40\%. On sparse mazes Manhattan
  is already near-exact, so the learned field's noise dominates; on dense mazes both
  heuristics expand many nodes and the relative penalty shrinks.
  \item \textbf{Size.} The ratio is \PfiveSizeTwentyRatio$\times$ at $N=20$,
  \PfiveSizeSixtyRatio$\times$ at $N=60$, and \PfiveSizeHundredRatio$\times$ at $N=100$.

  \item \textbf{Generator.} Backtracker mazes are worse (\PfiveBacktrackerRatio$\times$)

  \item \textbf{Tortuosity.} \PfiveDirectFraction{}\% of test mazes have tortuosity

  on the most tortuous mazes. The Spearman correlation between tortuosity and the log
  expansion ratio is \PfiveTortSpearman. When Manhattan is already optimal, there is no

\subsection{RQ4: Reproducibility Audit and the PyTorch Artefact}

The repository contains two model artefacts. The committed scikit-learn fallback
(\texttt{models/fallback\_mlp\_regressor.joblib}) is the model that produced the
headline numbers: its test $R^2$ is \PfiveRtwo{}, MAE \PfiveMae{} (\PfiveMaeCells{}
cells at $N=60$), Pearson $r=\PfivePearson$, Spearman $\rho=\PfiveSpearman$, macro
AUC-ROC \PfiveAuc{}, and macro $F_1$ \PfiveMacroFone{}. Regenerating the evaluation from
this artefact reproduces the committed search results, which is why all Phase~5
statistics are computed from that CSV.

The PyTorch checkpoint \texttt{models/neural\_heuristic\_seed42.pt} is \emph{not} a
trained model. Its metadata records \PfiveTorchEpochs{} epoch, at most
\PfiveTorchCells{} training cells per epoch, best epoch~1, and validation MAE
\PfiveTorchValMae{}. \texttt{scripts/run\_torch\_search.py} audits these fields and
refuses to benchmark the checkpoint by default; the audit is written to
\texttt{results/reproducibility/torch\_checkpoint\_audit.json}. When run with
\texttt{--allow-untrained} on 48 test mazes, the torch heuristic expands
\PfiveTorchNodes{} nodes (\PfiveTorchRelative$\times$ Manhattan), consistent with a
near-constant field rather than with the architecture's potential. This is a
reproducibility limitation, not evidence about the architecture: the PyTorch path is
unit-tested, but a meaningful comparison requires retraining
(\texttt{python -m src.train --epochs 30 --max-train-cells-per-epoch 0}).

\subsection{Discussion}

Why does a model with $R^2=\PfiveRtwo$ produce such poor search? Four factors compound.
\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{Objective mismatch.} MSE rewards average accuracy. A$^\ast$ is driven by
  the \emph{ordering} of $f=g+h$ and by admissibility along the optimal path. A field
  with low average error can still reorder the frontier catastrophically if its errors
  are spatially correlated, which they are.
  \item \textbf{Overestimation near the goal is expensive.} The raw field's positive
  bias near the goal is small in cell units but large relative to the remaining cost.
  It closes corridors and forces detours, which is why raw search is worse than even a
  constant heuristic on many mazes.
  \item \textbf{Clipping discards the learned signal.} $\min(\hat h,h_{\text{man}})$ is
  Manhattan wherever the prediction exceeds Manhattan. On direct mazes this is almost
  everywhere, so the clipped heuristic cannot beat the baseline by construction.
  \item \textbf{The representation is local.} A $5\times5$ window plus two goal offsets
  is a small receptive field. Neural A$^\ast$ \cite{yonetani2021} uses a convolutional
  encoder over the whole grid precisely because the value of a cell depends on global
  connectivity. Our ablation confirms that the wall window contributes little to
  regression and harms search.
\end{enumerate}

The negative result is therefore informative: it identifies the gap between
\emph{regression fidelity} and \emph{search usefulness}, and it shows that the gap is
not closed by more capacity or by clipping. A better system needs a global
representation, a search-aware loss (for example, penalising overestimation near the
goal or training against A$^\ast$ expansions), and an explicit consistency penalty.

\subsection{Threats to Validity}

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Single training run.} The fallback model was trained with one seed; the
  ablation retrains variants with the same seed, so between-seed variance is not
  quantified.
  \item \textbf{PyTorch artefact.} The committed torch checkpoint is a smoke test. All
  search claims use the scikit-learn model; the torch numbers are reported only as an
  audit.
  \item \textbf{Sampling.} Consistency and field diagnostics use 48 of 282 mazes and a
  5{,}000-cell cap per maze; the ablation uses 24 mazes for search impact. These samples
  are fixed by seed and are representative but not exhaustive.
  \item \textbf{Timing.} Runtimes are single-machine wall-clock measurements and are
  reported for context, not as a controlled benchmark.
  \item \textbf{Generator coverage.} Train and test share the same two generators and
  the same size/density grid; the conclusions do not automatically transfer to other
  maze families.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{RQ Answers and Phase Summary}

\begin{description}
  \item[RQ1.] No. Raw neural A$^\ast$ expands \PfiveRawRelative{}\% more nodes than
  Manhattan and wins on \PfiveRawWins{}\% of mazes; clipping restores near-parity
  (\PfiveClipRelative{}\%) but not an improvement.
  \item[RQ2.] Goal offsets carry almost all of the regression signal. Wall context adds
  about $0.01$ in $R^2$ but increases expansions; even the best feature subset is worse
  than Manhattan.
  \item[RQ3.] The failure is mechanical: \PfiveConsistencyRaw{}\% consistency
  violations, overestimation near the goal, underestimation far from it, and larger
  errors at dead ends and wall-adjacent cells. Clipping removes overestimation but
  deepens underestimation.
  \item[RQ4.] The scikit-learn artefact reproduces the reported results. The PyTorch
  checkpoint is a one-epoch smoke test and is explicitly refused by the benchmark script
  unless overridden.
\end{description}

The project therefore contributes a carefully measured negative result: a learned
heuristic that fits $h^\ast$ well in aggregate can still be a substantially worse search
heuristic than Manhattan, and the reason is visible in its consistency, bias, and
environment-dependent failure profile. These diagnostics, rather than the original
hypothesis, are the main empirical contribution of Deliverable~3.
